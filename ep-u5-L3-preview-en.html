This is the brief on fungi. So this unit covers mushrooms, molds, and yeasts, specifically focusing on how fungi are built, how they feed and reproduce, and the crucial partnerships they form. First, you might be wondering, what makes a fungus a fungus? Well, a fungus is a eukaryote with a chitin cell wall that feeds by absorbing nutrients from its surroundings because they literally cannot photosynthesize. Structurally, one thread is called a hypha, and many hyphae branch out into a mycelium. That mushroom you see is actually just the reproductive part above ground. For feeding, they use extracellular digestion, meaning they release enzymes into their food, digest it outside the body, and then just absorb the small molecules. Second, let's look at how they reproduce and group together. Fungi spread mainly by spores, right? But yeast also reproduces by budding, and a piece of mycelium can actually regrow through fragmentation. We've basically got three main groups here. Zygote fungi, which includes bread mold, sac fungi, which includes yeast and penicillium, and club fungi, which includes mushrooms. Finally, how do fungi interact with the world? As decomposers, they break down dead They form wild partnerships too, like mycorrhiza, where fungus threads extend a plant's root reach. The plant gets water and minerals, and the fungus gets sugar. Lichens are a fungus, plus an alga or cyanobacterium. The alga makes food, the fungus gives shelter, starting soil on bare rock. And as for useful fungi, yeast bakes bread and brews drinks, while penicillium gives us penicillin. Some fungi cause athlete's foot, ringworm, or spoil food.
