Transcript讲稿文字

This is the brief on four related terms from evolution. You know, individuals don't just evolve or change their genes to fit the environment. Rather, the population changes because some alleles become more common across generations. First up, the heritable trait. Basically, it's a trait that parents pass down to offspring through their genes. Just remember, heir equals inheritor. A heritable trait can be inherited. So can a scar from an injury be passed on to offspring? No, it's not caused by genes, so it's not heritable. Second is the allele, which is simply one version of a gene. Remember, allele sounds like other form. Gene equals recipe. Allele equals one version of the recipe. So what do we call different versions of the same gene? Alleles. Third, we have the gene pool. This refers to all the alleles found in all the members of a population. Think about it. Pool equals everything swimming in one place. So what does the gene pool of a population contain? All of its alleles. Fourth is allele frequency. This is just how common an allele is in a population, usually expressed as a percentage. Remember, frequency equals how often. Evolution at its smallest equals frequency changes. If allele A is found in 30 of 100 copies, what is its frequency? 30%. Now to connect this all, microevolution is a small change in allele frequencies within one population, while macroevolution is a big change over a long time, including new species and new groups. Let's test this out. Which is the original source of new alleles? Mutation. Mutations create new alleles. Selection only sorts them. And natural selection acts directly on what? Individuals. It favors individuals with helpful traits, but the population changes as a result.
