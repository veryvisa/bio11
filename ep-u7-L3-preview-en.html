This is the Brief on Animals Body Plans. We're breaking down the foundational blueprints of animal life, from body shapes to embryo development, showing you exactly how 95% of species, mostly invertebrates, and the rest of us are literally put together. So we're going to look at three core architectural steps today. Your external layout, internal layers, and structural support. First, external layout. Sponges have absolute asymmetry, meaning no matching halves. Jellyfish have radial symmetry. Many cuts through the center give you matching halves. But humans? We're bilateral, meaning only one cut gives mirror image left and right sides. Naturally, only bilateral animals have an anterior head, a posterior tail, a dorsal back, and a ventral belly. Imagine moving head first. It makes perfect sense to put all your sensors right at the front of the vehicle, a clustering we call cephalization. Second, internal building blocks. How does that shape even begin forming? Well, imagine pushing your thumb into a hollow ball of cells, the blastula. It folds inward into a gastrula, creating distinct layers. An outer ectoderm for skin, a middle mesoderm for muscles, and an inner endoderm for your gut. Diploblastic animals have two layers. Triploblastic have three. This creates cavities. Acellumates have none. Pseudocelumates are partially lined by mesoderm. And celumates have a fully lined, true celum. Finally, development and support. Here is the ultimate dividing line in animal evolution. Does your embryo's first opening, the blastopor, become your mouth or your bottom? If it's the mouth, like insects, you're a protostome. If it's the anus, like us vertebrates, you're a deuterostome. And for support, animals use hard exoskeletons, internal endoskeletons, or fluid-filled hydrostatic skeletons, kind of like an earthworm.
