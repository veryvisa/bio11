This is the brief on natural selection, fitness, adaptation, and selective pressure. These fundamental biology concepts describe how traits in populations change to suit their environment, demonstrating why this material matters for understanding evolution. First, let's look at natural selection. It's when individuals with traits suiting their environment survive and reproduce more, making those traits common.

Remember, nature does the selecting. Does natural selection change individuals or populations? Well, populations. It changes which traits are common. That brings us to fitness, which is how well an organism survives and reproduces compared with others of its kind.

In biology, fit means leaves more offspring, not strong. So, is the strongest animal always the fittest? No. Fitness is about leaving offspring. Second, let's talk about adaptation and selective pressure.

An adaptation is an inherited trait that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment. So, adapt equals fit to. An adaptation fits a body to its home. Now, you might be wondering, is a giraffe stretching its neck an adaptation? No.

An adaptation is an inherited trait, not a behavior done in one lifetime. This is driven by selective pressure, an environmental factor affecting which individuals survive and reproduce. Basically, pressure pushes the population in one direction. Name one selective pressure? Well, you've got predators, disease, food shortage, climate change, and so on.

Finally, here is how the idea fits together. The four steps are 1. Variation. Individuals differ. 2.

Inheritance. Some traits have passed on. 3. More offspring are born than can survive. And 4.

Individuals with helpful traits survive and breed more. So, which is the original source of new alleles? Mutation. Mutations create new alleles. Selection only sorts them.

To summarize, in natural selection, fitness means success at surviving and reproducing. Fitness is about leaving offspring, not strength.
