This is the brief on Unit 2, Evolution. We're running through BC Life Sciences 11, covering how populations change over generations, how new species form, and the evidence proving it. First, let's clear up a common misconception. Individuals don't evolve or change their genes to fit the environment. Instead, the population changes because certain alleles become more common across generations. Natural selection takes four exact steps. One, individuals naturally vary. Two, traits are inherited. Three, more offspring are born than can survive. And four, those with helpful traits survive and breed more. Take peppered moths. Soot darkened the trees, hiding dark moths from birds while light ones were eaten. The dark moths bred more, making dark the common color. Second, so how do new species actually form from these changes? It starts with microevolution, a small change in allele frequencies within one population. That leads to macroevolution, a massive change for a long time, including new species. In speciation, a physical barrier, like a river, splits a population. Each side collects different changes until they literally can't interbreed, becoming two separate species. Divergent evolution means relatives become different, while convergent evolution means non-relatives become similar in similar environments. Chance also changes allele frequencies through genetic drift. Whether a bottleneck shrinks a population, or a founder group starts fresh with a small sample of alleles. Finally, what are the four lines of evidence? Fossils show change through time. Homologous structures show shared ancestry despite doing different jobs, completely unlike analogous structures doing the same job from different ancestors. Embryos of vertebrates look alike, and DNA similarities match the family tree. This connects directly to artificial selection. People breed organisms with the traits they want. Wild cabbage became broccoli, cabbage, and kale this way.
